# Soundcheck — titles that argue

Build the argument. Make every title say its part. Read the whole thing aloud. Seven principles for decks and documents, by Nicolas Chan; the default for every TI deck and document built here. Hold 1 and 2 most strongly, use 3–5 as strong defaults, aim for 6, reach for 7.

**The one rule.** Don't use the title to describe what is on the slide. Use it to state the point the slide is making. The slide underneath exists to demonstrate, explain or prove that statement. A slide's title is its governing thought; a deck has one too.

## Working order

- Phase A, find the structure: start with principle 7, draft the title spine (every title, in order, nothing else), apply 1–3 roughly, then build the slides. For each spine claim, identify the support you have or the gap.
- Phase B, sharpen: apply 1 and 2, then 3–5, trim with 6, re-read the titles together. Revisit the story once the material is assembled.
- Evidence may come from world knowledge, research or the user's material. Distinguish established knowledge, sourced findings, inference and missing support. Never present an inference as a verified result; never invent a fact to strengthen a sentence.

## The seven principles

1. **Make a claim, not a label.** A label names the subject ("Our manufacturing footprint"). A claim tells us something about it ("TI owns its manufacturing, so supply doesn't depend on a foundry's queue"). Ask: if a reader saw only this line, what would they now know? And: does the title survive the audience looking at the slide?
2. **Make the claim carry the why.** Ask "so what, to the person in the room?" Ladder: "Q3 performance" → "Q3 revenue grew 22%" → "Q3 grew 22%, and almost all of it came from one channel." Stop when the significance is clear for this slide's job.
3. **One slide, one point, as far as possible.** Count ideas and relationships, not clauses or conjunctions; "and" is not a litmus test. A cause and effect, a contrast or a trade-off can need two clauses to make one point.
4. **Write for the ear.** Write something a person could say to a colleague, then read it aloud. "Heavy media spend on LinkedIn to reach key demographic of Deciders" becomes "Put the campaign in front of the people choosing suppliers."
5. **Make the case fully.** Be specific about the conclusion and the decisive reason. Anticipate "why this? why not the alternative? what about…?" Keep the qualifications that materially change the claim.
6. **Say it in as few words as possible.** Soundcheck has no word or character count. A longer title that lands the argument is worth more than an elegant line that loses it. The layout's ≤ 8-word budget is a layout budget: when the strongest supported title collides with it, flag the collision and offer a shorter title or a layout change.
7. **Read end to end: the titles tell one story.** Read only the titles. They should form a coherent argument, almost a continuous paragraph. This is the Title Test. Look for the missing bridge, the detour, two competing arguments, the missing why.

## Where the principles relax

Covers, section dividers, agendas, laundry lists, reference appendices, back-up and methodology slides. "Summary" and "Next steps" are deprioritised, not banned.

## Deck-side additions

- One voice move per headline: don't stack a question, a coloured word and a strikethrough on one line.
- Colour the emphasis rather than italicising it: one `teal-deck` bold phrase inside an `ink-strong` line, or the whole title in `title` red. Contrast is still the gate.
- Improvements, not scores. Offer strong alternatives when a choice helps; keep good titles and say why; respect editing scope; re-check audience and story at the end. Never claim layout fit or factual verification that was not performed.
